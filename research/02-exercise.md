# 02 — L-Carnitine in Exercise Physiology and Muscle

Dossier for the exercise/muscle evidence stream. Every factual claim carries an inline footnote; verbatim quotes backing load-bearing claims (programmatically extracted from the saved pages in `research/exercise/pages/`) live in `research/findings_exercise.json`.

## 1. Overview

Carnitine has two exercise roles: translocation of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria via the carnitine palmitoyltransferase (CPT) system, and buffering of excess acetyl groups as acetylcarnitine to sustain the free CoA pool that pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) and TCA flux require (New insights concerning the role of carnitine in the regulation of fuel metabolism in skeletal muscle — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17331998/, accessed 2026-11-15). At high intensity, acetylation consumes free carnitine and fat oxidation falls (The effects of increasing exercise intensity on muscle fuel utilisation in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11579177/, accessed 2026-11-15).

Total muscle carnitine resists change: 4–6 g/day oral for up to 14 days — and even intravenous carnitine — raise plasma but leave muscle untouched (Effect of L-carnitine supplementation on muscle and blood carnitine content — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7987362/, accessed 2026-11-15; Effect of intravenous L-carnitine on carnitine homeostasis and fuel metabolism during exercise in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8004884/, accessed 2026-11-15). The proven loading strategy pairs chronic L-carnitine L-tartrate (LCLT) with insulin-stimulating carbohydrate: 2 g LCLT plus 80 g carbohydrate twice daily for 24 weeks raised muscle total carnitine 21%, spared glycogen at low intensity, reduced lactate at high intensity, lowered perceived exertion, and increased 30-minute work output 11% (Chronic oral ingestion of L-carnitine and carbohydrate increases muscle carnitine content and alters muscle fuel metabolism during exercise in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21224234/, accessed 2026-11-15).

As an acute ergogenic the evidence is mostly null, with recent reviews seeing endurance improvements only under chronic dosing and no support for "fat-burning" claims (Carnitine and sports medicine: use or abuse? — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15591004/, accessed 2026-11-15; Acute and Chronic L-Carnitine Supplementation Enhances Endurance Performance Without Consistent Effects on Substrate Oxidation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42887880/, accessed 2026-11-15). The most consistent positive evidence is muscle-damage/recovery: ~2 g/day LCLT attenuates CK, myoglobin, oxidative-stress and soreness markers and upregulates androgen receptors (l-Carnitine l-tartrate supplementation favorably affects biochemical markers of recovery — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20045157/, accessed 2026-11-15; Androgenic responses to resistance exercise — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16826026/, accessed 2026-11-15). The thru-hike context — months of low-intensity work in large energy deficit — is fat-dominant metabolism (Regulation of endogenous fat and carbohydrate metabolism in relation to exercise intensity and duration — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8214047/, accessed 2026-11-15; Low Energy Availability and Increased Risk of RED-S During a 3767-km Thru-Hike — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37586947/, accessed 2026-11-15), but no carnitine trial in thru-hikers exists. Human heat/thermoregulation data are absent; the strongest heat-stress evidence is a mouse study in which L-carnitine pretreatment protected against heat-stress acute kidney injury (l-Carnitine pretreatment ameliorates heat stress-induced acute kidney injury — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38095023/, accessed 2026-11-15).

## 2. Evidence in depth

### 2.1 Carnitine's exercise roles (mechanism)

Resting muscle total carnitine (~20 mmol/kg dry mass) is ~77% free and ~19% acetylcarnitine; intense effort converts free carnitine to acetylcarnitine — the buffer of excess acetyl units (Acetylcarnitine formation during intense muscular contraction in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3624147/, accessed 2026-11-15). When intensity reaches 75% of maximal workload, whole-body fat oxidation falls as PDC activation and carnitine acetylation deplete free carnitine; the likely mechanism is CPT1 downregulation from that decline (or falling pH) (The effects of increasing exercise intensity on muscle fuel utilisation in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11579177/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2018 molecular-physiology review locates the intensity switch at mitochondrial fatty-acyl import: acetyl-group sequestration by carnitine determines carnitine availability for the CPT1 reaction (Molecular Regulation of Fatty Acid Oxidation in Skeletal Muscle during Aerobic Exercise — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29221849/, accessed 2026-11-15). Muscle cannot synthesize carnitine and depends entirely on blood uptake (Carnitine in Human Muscle Bioenergetics — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31906370/, accessed 2026-11-15). Two recent trials measured this arm directly: 36 days of 2 g/day in impaired-glucose-tolerant volunteers raised post-exercise acetylcarnitine and lowered long-chain acylcarnitines (Carnitine supplementation improves metabolic flexibility — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31676389/, accessed 2026-11-15), and 12 weeks of 2970 mg/day in type 2 diabetes elevated resting and post-exercise acetylcarnitine with improved insulin sensitivity (Carnitine supplementation improves insulin sensitivity and skeletal muscle acetylcarnitine formation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40019115/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 2.2 Muscle loading: insulin co-stimulation is the operative variable

Insulin acutely stimulates OCTN2-mediated muscle carnitine accumulation: 5 h of intravenous L-carnitine during hyperinsulinemia raised muscle total carnitine from 22.0 to 24.7 mmol/kg dry muscle, with no effect at fasting insulin (Insulin stimulates L-carnitine accumulation in human skeletal muscle — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16368715/, accessed 2026-11-15). The effect has a threshold — only serum insulin ≥90 mU/L stimulates accumulation (A threshold exists for the stimulatory effect of insulin on plasma L-carnitine clearance in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17047160/, accessed 2026-11-15) — and carbohydrate co-ingestion augments retention (Carbohydrate ingestion augments L-carnitine retention in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17138832/, accessed 2026-11-15), while added protein acutely blunts uptake (Protein ingestion acutely inhibits insulin-stimulated muscle carnitine uptake — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26675771/, accessed 2026-11-15).

Without that insulin signal, loading fails across every design: 14 days of 4 g/day (Effect of L-carnitine supplementation on muscle and blood carnitine content — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7987362/, accessed 2026-11-15), 6 g/day for 7–14 days (Carnitine supplementation: effect on muscle carnitine and glycogen content — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7808246/, accessed 2026-11-15), intravenous dosing during exercise (Effect of intravenous L-carnitine — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8004884/, accessed 2026-11-15), 3 months of 2×2 g/day (Long-term administration of L-carnitine to humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11880112/, accessed 2026-11-15), and 24 weeks of 1 g/day LCLT with the insulin secretagogue leucine, which raised plasma but not muscle carnitine (Twenty-Four Weeks of L-Carnitine Combined with Leucine Supplementation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36696886/, accessed 2026-11-15).

The successful protocol (Wall et al. 2011): 14 recreational athletes, randomized double-blind, 2 g LCLT (1.36 g L-carnitine; Carnipure) + 80 g carbohydrate twice daily for 24 weeks. Muscle total carnitine rose 21%; at 50% VO2max the loaded group used 55% less glycogen with 31% less PDC activation; at 80% VO2max PDC activation was 38% higher, lactate 44% lower, PCr/ATP better maintained; work output rose 11% (Chronic oral ingestion of L-carnitine and carbohydrate — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21224234/, accessed 2026-11-15). Perceived exertion at 80% VO2max fell (Borg 14.0 vs 16.2) — the full text states these effects "resulted in a reduced perception of effort and increased work output during a validated exercise performance test" (Chronic oral ingestion of L-carnitine and carbohydrate (full text) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3060373/, accessed 2026-11-15). The brief's "roughly 10–20%" hint verifies as 21% at 24 weeks (~15% acutely with insulin-clamped IV infusion; An acute increase in skeletal muscle carnitine content alters fuel metabolism — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16984983/, accessed 2026-11-15). A companion trial (12 weeks) loaded muscle 20%, raised energy expenditure 6% and long-chain acyl-CoA 200%, and prevented the 1.8 kg fat-mass gain of the carbohydrate-only control (Skeletal muscle carnitine loading increases energy expenditure — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23818692/, accessed 2026-11-15). Counterpoint: verified loading (muscle free carnitine 30–40% higher) combined with 24 weeks of high-intensity interval training added nothing to adaptations or performance gains (Increasing skeletal muscle carnitine availability does not alter the adaptations to high-intensity interval training — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28345160/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 2.3 Performance trials

Two decades of supplementation research produced no compelling evidence of improved performance in healthy subjects (Carnitine and sports medicine: use or abuse? — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15591004/, accessed 2026-11-15). A February 2026 systematic review of 18 randomized trials (acute 2–15 g; chronic 2–3 g/day for 2–24 weeks) concluded both acute and chronic dosing were associated with improved endurance performance, while fat oxidation during exercise was unchanged, the "fat-burning" reputation is unsupported, and no included trial was at low risk of bias (Acute and Chronic L-Carnitine Supplementation Enhances Endurance Performance — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42887880/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2021 meta-analysis of 30 publications found no lactate differences but significant changes in resting VO2 and serum carnitine (Clinical Effects of L-Carnitine Supplementation on Physical Performance — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34842765/, accessed 2026-11-15). Acute dosing remains null: 3 g LCLT 90 minutes before a CrossFit workout changed neither repetitions, RPE, nor blood pressure (Acute L-Carnitine Supplementation Does Not Improve CrossFit Performance — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40944177/, accessed 2026-11-15). Chronic supplementation elevates muscle carnitine only with carbohydrates, and raises fasting plasma TMAO — the counterpoint for the TMAO stream (The bright and the dark sides of L-carnitine supplementation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32958033/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 2.4 Muscle damage and recovery (Kraemer program)

Twenty-one days of 2 g/day L-carnitine as LCLT upregulated pre-exercise androgen receptor content in resistance-trained men (12.9 vs 11.2 au), which the authors suggest may promote recovery (Androgenic responses to resistance exercise — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16826026/, accessed 2026-11-15). Dose-finding showed 1 g and 2 g/day both reduced post-exercise hypoxanthine, xanthine oxidase, myoglobin and perceived soreness, with 2 g giving the highest serum carnitine (Responses of criterion variables to different supplemental doses of L-carnitine L-tartrate — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17313301/, accessed 2026-11-15). In middle-aged adults, 2 g/day for ~3 weeks attenuated hypoxanthine, xanthine oxidase, malondialdehyde, myoglobin, creatine kinase and soreness, while strength and power markers were unaffected (l-Carnitine l-tartrate supplementation favorably affects biochemical markers of recovery — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20045157/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2020 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs found soreness improved at all five time points (0–96 h) and CK, myoglobin and LDH reduced at 24 h only, with benefits concentrated in resistance-training and untrained populations (The Effect of L-Carnitine Supplementation on Exercise-Induced Muscle Damage — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154768/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2021 industry-funded RCT (n=80, 5 weeks) improved perceived recovery/soreness (p = 0.021), lowered CK (p = 0.016) and raised superoxide dismutase (L-Carnitine Tartrate Supplementation for 5 Weeks Improves Exercise Recovery — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684429/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2023 scoping review concludes carnitine "could help in postexercise recovery" while mechanisms remain unresolved (Effects of L-Carnitine Intake on Exercise-Induced Muscle Damage — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37299549/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 2.5 Prolonged exertion and the thru-hike context

Peripheral lipolysis is maximal at the lowest intensities and plasma fatty-acid release falls as intensity rises — all-day, low-to-moderate hiking is fat-dominant (Regulation of endogenous fat and carbohydrate metabolism — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8214047/, accessed 2026-11-15). Documented thru-hikes run large deficits: a 55-year-old man averaged 5702 kcal/day expenditure against 4141 kcal/day intake over 128 days on the Pacific Crest Trail — energy availability 19.3 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day (low), 9% body-mass loss, 46% fat-mass loss, moderate RED-S risk (Low Energy Availability and Increased Risk of RED-S — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37586947/, accessed 2026-11-15). PCT case studies corroborate the pattern and add deleterious findings — reduced flow-mediated dilation after a 112-day hike on a "calorie-rich, nutrient-poor" diet, and lumbar BMD losses despite load-bearing activity (Impact of a long-distance hike on the Pacific Crest Trail — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33661563/, accessed 2026-11-15; Body composition and bone changes following a thru-hike — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42572415/, accessed 2026-11-15). The carnitine link remains indirect: Wall 2011's glycogen sparing occurred at 50% VO2max — the thru-hike intensity domain — but no study has administered carnitine to thru-hikers.

### 2.6 Heat and thermoregulation (thin — flagged gap)

Patients with severe heat-related illness and/or AKI showed elevated serum acylcarnitine, and in heat-stressed mice L-carnitine pretreatment restored tubular mitochondrial ATP production and prevented the proinflammatory macrophage shift of AKI (l-Carnitine pretreatment ameliorates heat stress-induced acute kidney injury — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38095023/, accessed 2026-11-15). The rest is livestock: L-carnitine ameliorated heat-stress disruptions in rams via antioxidant capacity (Exogenous L-Carnitine Administration Ameliorates the Adverse Effects of Heat Stress — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35464382/, accessed 2026-11-15). No human trial of carnitine for thermoregulation, sweat handling, or heat tolerance during exercise was found; one related broiler trial was retracted and excluded.

## 3. Human evidence (trial quality)

The loading trials are small but mechanistically deep: Wall 2011 (14 recreational athletes, biopsy endpoints; Chronic oral ingestion of L-carnitine and carbohydrate — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21224234/, accessed 2026-11-15); Wall 2013 (12); the Stephens clamp studies (7–8 men each, intravenous delivery). The 2026 systematic review rated no included trial at low risk of bias (Acute and Chronic L-Carnitine Supplementation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42887880/, accessed 2026-11-15). The Kraemer LCLT trials are crossover designs of 8–18 adults at a consistent 2 g/day dose; conflicts to disclose: the 2021 recovery trial was funded by Lonza Consumer Health with employee co-authors (L-Carnitine Tartrate Supplementation for 5 Weeks — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34684429/, accessed 2026-11-15) and two authors of the 2025 diabetes trial are Alfasigma employees (Carnitine supplementation improves insulin sensitivity — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40019115/, accessed 2026-11-15). Thru-hike data are single-subject case studies without carnitine outcomes; the heat evidence is one mouse study plus a clinical association.

## 4. What is NOT established

- Acute "fat-burning" ergogenesis: not supported by current evidence (Acute and Chronic L-Carnitine Supplementation Enhances Endurance Performance — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42887880/, accessed 2026-11-15; Acute L-Carnitine Supplementation Does Not Improve CrossFit Performance — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40944177/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Benefits of loading for trained athletes: 24 weeks of verified loading added nothing to HIIT adaptations (Increasing skeletal muscle carnitine availability does not alter the adaptations — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28345160/, accessed 2026-11-15); Wall 2011's +11% work output was in recreational athletes and remains unreplicated.
- Muscle-damage benefits beyond resistance exercise/untrained populations, beyond 24 h for blood markers, and their mechanisms (The Effect of L-Carnitine Supplementation on Exercise-Induced Muscle Damage — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32154768/, accessed 2026-11-15; Effects of L-Carnitine Intake on Exercise-Induced Muscle Damage — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37299549/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Transfer to eccentric hiking damage or to actual thru-hikers: no trial exists.
- Carnitine and human thermoregulation/heat tolerance: no human data (murine and livestock only).
- Renal protection in exercising humans under heat: not tested; the murine pretreatment data are hypothesis-generating only.
- Reconciliation of chronic-supplementation TMAO elevation with the renal-support framing (The bright and the dark sides of L-carnitine supplementation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32958033/, accessed 2026-11-15) — must be resolved with the TMAO stream, not hidden.

## 6. Open questions for the parent

1. **Loading timeline vs trail season.** Verified loading requires 12–24 weeks of LCLT plus ~80 g carbohydrate twice daily (~600 extra kcal/day) — a pre-season window; on-trail loading will not finish before a hike ends.
2. **Dose reconciliation.** Recovery trials use 2 g/day LCLT without carbohydrate requirements; loading requires carbohydrate co-ingestion and avoids protein at the loading drink (Protein ingestion acutely inhibits insulin-stimulated muscle carnitine uptake — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26675771/, accessed 2026-11-15) — coordinate with the practical stream for one protocol table.
3. **Does glycogen sparing matter in energy deficit?** Wall 2011's sparing was measured in fed recreational athletes; thru-hikers run ~1500 kcal/day deficits (Low Energy Availability and Increased Risk of RED-S — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37586947/, accessed 2026-11-15) where fat oxidation already dominates — present sparing as mechanistic plausibility, not a field-proven benefit.
4. **Heat chapter depends on the renal stream.** The Goto 2024 mouse data is the only direct carnitine–heat–kidney evidence; the renal-support claim itself belongs in the renal stream, labeled translational.
5. **Quote-handling caveats.** efetch abstracts drop the VO2max symbol (quotes read "At 50% , the Carnitine group…"); full-text quotes carry PMC markdown italics ("_vs._", "_P_"). Whitespace must be collapsed before substring verification; the saved page files are ground truth.
