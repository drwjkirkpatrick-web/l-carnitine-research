# 06 — Practical, Dosing, and Safety

Dossier for the practical/safety stream: trail form, dose, schedule and cost; tolerability; drug interactions (coumarins, thyroid hormone, valproate); renal cautions; contraindication scanning; trail-logistics gaps. Load-bearing claims carry verbatim quotes from the saved pages (`research/practical/pages/`) in `research/findings_practical.json`.

## 1. Overview

The on-disk evidence supports this working answer: **L-carnitine L-tartrate (LCLT) powder at ~2 g/day, split into two doses taken with carbohydrate-containing meals** — the form and dose with the most consistent recovery data; muscle loading additionally requires months of daily carbohydrate co-ingestion. It is cheap (~$0.13/day as bulk powder) and well tolerated below ~3 g/day, with four cautions: coumarin anticoagulant users (INR destabilization), thyroid-hormone replacement patients (carnitine antagonizes thyroid hormone action at 2–4 g/day), seizure disorders, and renal impairment. Desert-heat stability of the powder has no data on disk — state the gap, don't extrapolate.

## 2. Dosing and forms

### 2.1 The 2 g/day LCLT recovery literature

The recovery trials standardized on 2 g/day. In resistance-trained men, three weeks of 2 g/day LCLT reduced exercise-induced muscle tissue damage on MRI of the thigh (The effects of L-carnitine L-tartrate supplementation on hormonal responses to resistance exercise and recovery — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930169/, accessed 2026-11-15). In middle-aged adults, 2 g/day (Carnipure tartrate, as total dose of l-carnitine) attenuated purine-metabolism and free-radical markers, myoglobin, creatine kinase and soreness after a squat/leg-press challenge, with strength and power unaffected (l-Carnitine l-tartrate supplementation favorably affects biochemical markers of recovery from physical exertion in middle-aged men and women — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20045157/, accessed 2026-11-15). Operative lesson: ~2 g/day for weeks buys recovery biochemistry, not acute performance.

### 2.2 Bioavailability and split dosing

Only about 14–18% of a supplemental oral dose is absorbed, versus 63–75% of dietary carnitine (Carnitine - Health Professional Fact Sheet — https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/carnitine-HealthProfessional/, accessed 2026-11-15). The fraction falls further as the bolus grows: in a dose-escalation study (0, 0.5, 1, 2 g three times daily), plasma carnitine rose significantly at the 0.5 g dose but showed no further increase at higher doses — nonlinearity above 0.5 g three times a day (Disposition and metabolite kinetics of oral L-carnitine in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16988205/, accessed 2026-11-15), from saturable renal tubular reabsorption (Carnitine and acylcarnitines: pharmacokinetic, pharmacological and clinical aspects — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22804748/, accessed 2026-11-15). The FDA label divides the adult dose the same way: 990 mg two or three times daily (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 2.3 Carbohydrate co-ingestion and the acute/chronic distinction

Muscle resists loading unless insulin is recruited: the one successful dietary loading protocol gave 2 g LCLT plus 80 g carbohydrate twice daily for 24 weeks — muscle total carnitine rose 21%, glycogen use at 50% VO2max fell 55%, work output rose 11% — and **all significant effects occurred only after 24 weeks** (Chronic oral ingestion of L-carnitine and carbohydrate increases muscle carnitine content and alters muscle fuel metabolism during exercise in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21224234/, accessed 2026-11-15). Carbohydrate ingestion augments carnitine retention via insulin (Carbohydrate ingestion augments L-carnitine retention in humans — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17138832/, accessed 2026-11-15). Translation: carnitine goes with meals — the carbohydrate context the loading evidence requires — and a first dose promises nothing: single doses move plasma, not muscle.

### 2.4 Other forms

Acetyl-L-carnitine (ALC) is the CNS-endpoint form: meta-analysis of twelve RCTs (791 participants) found ALC significantly decreased depressive symptoms versus placebo, matching antidepressants with fewer adverse effects, in trials of 1–3 g/day over 12–52 weeks (Acetyl-L-Carnitine Supplementation and the Treatment of Depressive Symptoms: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29076953/, accessed 2026-11-15; Carnitine - Health Professional Fact Sheet — https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/carnitine-HealthProfessional/, accessed 2026-11-15). But acetylcarnitine has significantly lower bioavailability than carnitine, and ~90% of both was metabolized to TMAO, reaching ~50 µM plasma levels previously associated with adverse outcomes (Low Bioavailability and High TMAO Production: Novel Insights Into Acetylcarnitine and Carnitine Metabolism — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41243468/, accessed 2026-11-15). Glycine propionyl-L-carnitine (GPLC) is the one form with an acute signal: 4.5 g 90 minutes before repeated Wingate sprints enhanced peak power with less lactate accumulation (Glycine propionyl-L-carnitine produces enhanced anaerobic work capacity with reduced lactate accumulation in resistance trained males — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19341458/, accessed 2026-11-15).

## 3. Safety and interactions

### 3.1 Tolerability ceiling and the fishy-odor problem

Carnitine has **no established tolerable upper intake level**; ~3 g/day can cause nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, and a fishy body odor (Carnitine - Health Professional Fact Sheet — https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/carnitine-HealthProfessional/, accessed 2026-11-15). The FDA label attributes GI complaints (transient nausea and vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea) to long-term oral use, states decreasing the dosage often diminishes or eliminates body odor or GI symptoms, and advises tolerance monitoring during the first week and after dose increases (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15). The odor is trimethylamine — gut microbes convert unabsorbed carnitine to TMA, which carries the characteristic fishy odour (The complex metabolism of trimethylamine in humans: endogenous and exogenous sources — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27126549/, accessed 2026-11-15). Offsetting caution: prolonged supplementation elevates fasting plasma TMAO — a compound supposed to be pro-atherogenic — without detectable inflammatory or oxidative-stress changes (The bright and the dark sides of L-carnitine supplementation: a systematic review — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32958033/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### 3.2 The D,L-carnitine contamination lesson

The scariest historical signal is not about pure L-carnitine. In 1979–1984, dialysis patients on that era's carnitine preparations developed a myasthenia-like syndrome: severe weakness of cranial and girdle muscles in 4 of 20 dialysis patients, with impaired neuromuscular transmission suggesting hemicholinium-like synaptic activity (Myasthenia due to carnitine treatment — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7381520/, accessed 2026-11-15). The original report from the same group described the same syndrome on long-term haemodialysis (Myasthenia-like syndrome associated with carnitine in patients on long-term haemodialysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/86768/, accessed 2026-11-15), and a French case tied it explicitly to D,L-carnitine ([Myasthenic syndrome induced by D,L-carnitine in a chronic hemodialysis patient] — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6232557/, accessed 2026-11-15). The FDA label is categorical: **mild myasthenia has been described only in uremic patients receiving D,L-carnitine** (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15). Modern pharmaceutical levocarnitine is the pure L-isomer; use products specified as L-carnitine/LCLT (label-accuracy risk: see §4).

### 3.3 Seizures

Seizures have been reported in patients with or without pre-existing seizure activity receiving oral or intravenous levocarnitine, with increased frequency/severity in those with existing seizures (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15). No dose threshold is given — flag epilepsy patients rather than set limits.

### 3.4 Coumarin anticoagulants: the most operationally important interaction

This interaction carries label-level authority: the label directs INR monitoring when levocarnitine is started or dose-adjusted in warfarin patients, because reports of INR increase exist (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15). The professional monograph is moderate: limited data suggest levocarnitine may potentiate warfarin's hypoprothrombinemic effects, with isolated case reports of INR increase and/or bleeding (INR normalizing after discontinuation); the mechanism is unknown (Drug Interactions between levocarnitine and warfarin (Drugs.com) — https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/levocarnitine-with-warfarin-1454-0-2311-0.html?professional=1, accessed 2026-11-15). The anchor case: a 33-year-old with a mechanical aortic valve, stable for a year on acenocoumarol (INR 1.99–2.94), developed an INR rise to 4.65 after ten weeks of 1000 mg/day carnitine in a fitness drink; INR restabilized (2.4–2.8) for four-plus months after the product was omitted (Interaction of food supplement L-carnitine with oral anticoagulant acenocoumarol — https://smw.ch/index.php/smw/article/download/389/386/766, accessed 2026-11-15). An earlier letter reported the same potentiation (Potentiation of acenocoumarol action by L-carnitine — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8429297/, accessed 2026-11-15). Counterpoint: a small ICU RCT found 3 g/day enteral L-carnitine for one week *decreased* INR versus placebo (The effects of adjunctive treatment with L-carnitine on monitoring laboratory variables in ICU patients — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36597167/, accessed 2026-11-15) — INR can move either direction, which strengthens monitoring over directional prediction. Bottom line: anticoagulated thru-hikers need an INR check within two weeks of starting carnitine.

### 3.5 Thyroid: dose-dependent peripheral antagonism

L-carnitine is a naturally occurring peripheral antagonist of thyroid hormone action, inhibiting T3 and T4 entry into cell nuclei (Effects of carnitine on thyroid hormone action — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15591013/, accessed 2026-11-15), validated in a randomized double-blind trial in 50 TSH-suppressed women: 2 or 4 g/day oral L-carnitine reversed and prevented hyperthyroid symptoms and benefited bone mineralization (Usefulness of L-carnitine, a naturally occurring peripheral antagonist of thyroid hormone action, in iatrogenic hyperthyroidism — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11502782/, accessed 2026-11-15). Follow-up: a pilot in subclinical hyperthyroidism used 500 mg/day carnitine plus 83 mcg selenium with symptom relief that reversed off treatment (A novel treatment for subclinical hyperthyroidism: a pilot study on the beneficial effects of l-carnitine and selenium — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28537653/, accessed 2026-11-15), and a randomized trial in overt Graves' disease found adjunctive carnitine + selenium lowered required methimazole dosage and hastened TRAb negativity (Adding L-Carnitine and Selenium to Methimazole in Graves' Disease — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40871722/, accessed 2026-11-15). Flip side: a hiker on levothyroxine taking 2–4 g/day for weeks could blunt thyroid hormone action — a feature in hyperthyroidism, a hazard on T4.

### 3.6 Valproate

Meta-analysis: blood ammonia is significantly higher and blood carnitine significantly lower in VPA-treated versus non-VPA groups; measure both, especially at high VPA dosages (Blood Levels of Ammonia and Carnitine in Patients Treated with Valproic Acid: A Meta-analysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35879038/, accessed 2026-11-15). Reviews support supplemental carnitine in VPA-induced hyperammonemia/hepatotoxicity: not harmful, commonly recommended in severe VPA poisoning especially in children (liver-protection benefit not clearly established), with prophylactic supplementation advocated in high-risk pediatric patients (Carnitine in the treatment of valproic acid-induced toxicity — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19280426/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2025 review recommends IV dosing (5 mg/kg loading dose plus infusion) on PK grounds, citing 14–18% oral bioavailability and saturable renal reabsorption (L-carnitine for valproic acid-induced toxicity — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39261302/, accessed 2026-11-15), while a 2026 poisonings systematic review found LC/ALC generally well tolerated but no kinetic or survival advantage in a valproate cohort (L-Carnitine and Acetyl-L-Carnitine in Drug Poisonings — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41692009/, accessed 2026-11-15). Rare on trail, but unsupervised self-dosing in this group is unwise.

### 3.7 Renal function: state the paradox plainly

Oral levocarnitine's safety and efficacy have not been evaluated in patients with renal insufficiency, and chronic high doses in severe renal impairment or ESRD may result in accumulation of the potentially toxic metabolites trimethylamine (TMA) and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), normally excreted in urine (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15) — yet dialysis patients are the one population with an approved carnitine indication, dosed 10–20 mg/kg IV after each dialysis session guided by trough levels (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE injection, solution — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=2685d8da-010e-40d7-914f-80546634888d, accessed 2026-11-15). Cochrane's verdict across 52 RCTs (3398 dialysis participants): the evidence does not support carnitine for dialysis-related carnitine deficiency, and it made little or no difference to adverse events (Carnitine supplements for people with chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36472884/, accessed 2026-11-15). In a CKD rat model, carnitine worsened disease progression, mineral metabolism and cortical porosity without physical-function benefit (Musculoskeletal Health Worsened from Carnitine Supplementation and Not Impacted by a Novel Individualized Treadmill Training Protocol — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38377965/, accessed 2026-11-15). Bottom line: normal renal function at ≤2 g/day needs no CKD-specific caution beyond the label's; CKD hikers should not self-dose — the oral label excludes them, and the one direct animal study was negative.

### 3.8 Pregnancy, lactation, pediatric

Animal studies up to 3.8× the human dose showed no fetal harm, but there are no adequate well-controlled studies in pregnant women and the label sanctions use only if clearly needed; nursing-mother supplementation has not been specifically studied (DailyMed - LEVOCARNITINE tablet — https://www.dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=0f749812-8f02-4eeb-91d9-5dd6fc9fa159, accessed 2026-11-15). Pediatric dosing covers deficiency states only (50–100 mg/kg/day divided, max 3 g/day). The practical exclusion list: warfarin users (until INR is checked), T4-replacement patients at gram doses, seizure patients, CKD patients, and pregnancy.

## 4. What is NOT established (gaps, failed hints)

- **Heat stability of LCLT powder under desert conditions: NOT FOUND** — no saved page addresses dry LCLT at pack temperatures; the nearest analogs are acetyl-L-carnitine in 5% dextrose — stable at neutral-to-acidic pH at room temperature for at least 33 days, unstable above pH 9 (Stability of acetyl-1-carnitine in 5% dextrose using a high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry times 2 method — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23050330/, accessed 2026-11-15) — and acylcarnitine degradation in room-temperature-stored dried blood spots (Instability of Acylcarnitines in Stored Dried Blood Spots — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33147805/, accessed 2026-11-15). Neither approximates 40 °C desert storage of dry powder; present cool/dry/sealed storage as an unvalidated precaution.
- **Supplement label-accuracy/quality surveys: NOT FOUND on disk.** Retail pages give price and package data only.
- **No tolerable upper intake level exists** (confirmed absent by NIH ODS); any "ceiling" must be framed as the ~3 g/day symptom threshold, not a UL.
- **Split versus single daily dosing: no head-to-head trial** — inferred from PK nonlinearity and label convention.
- **The "1979/1980s Japanese experiences" hint: not on disk as such.** The saved cases are Italian (Lancet 1979; J Neurol Sci 1980) and French (Presse Med 1984); no Japanese primary report.
- **No thru-hike-specific dosing data.** The 2 g/day evidence is resistance exercise; the 24-week loading protocol is impractical mid-hike.
- **TMAO long-term cardiovascular outcomes: unresolved.** Levels are documented; outcome evidence is not.

## 6. Open questions for the parent

- **Form decision:** adopt LCLT powder 2 g/day split BID with meals as the trail default?
- **Loading scope:** include the 24-week loading protocol, or limit trail recommendations to the ≤3-week recovery-dose evidence?
- **Warfarin box:** carry a boxed INR caution for anticoagulated hikers (Bachmann: INR 4.65 at 1000 mg/day × 10 weeks, restabilized off supplement)?
- **Thyroid framing:** hazard note for levothyroxine users, therapeutic note for hyperthyroidism — or both?
- **CKD paragraph:** state the dialysis paradox explicitly, or keep it brief?
- **Heat stability and product quality:** print NOT FOUND rather than extrapolate; want one additional fetch (assay-verified product) before naming one commercially?
- **Pediatric/pregnancy:** keep out of trail recommendations entirely, per label status.
