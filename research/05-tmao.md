## 1. Overview

The gut-microbiome TMAO pathway is the strongest published argument against chronic L-carnitine supplementation, and this stream maps it at full strength — then weighs it. The chain: oral L-carnitine (like choline and phosphatidylcholine) is converted by gut bacteria to trimethylamine (TMA); absorbed TMA is oxidized by hepatic flavin monooxygenase 3 (FMO3) to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), excreted in urine (FMO3-Mediated Host-Microbiome Metabolic Axis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27190056/, accessed 2026-11-15). Production is microbiome-dependent: the post-challenge TMAO rise was "markedly suppressed after the administration of antibiotics and then reappeared after withdrawal" (Intestinal microbial metabolism of phosphatidylcholine and cardiovascular risk — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23614584/, accessed 2026-11-15). Established in humans: omnivores produce far more TMAO from an oral carnitine challenge than vegans/vegetarians, and supplement-dose carnitine raises fasting plasma TMAO. Everything downstream — cardiovascular events, CKD progression — is association-grade, contested by Mendelian randomization, null results in the sickest populations, and the fish problem. For a healthy athlete with normal kidney function, no saved source demonstrates renal or other harm from oral carnitine at supplement doses; the honest position is real metabolic exposure, unproven harm, residual uncertainty.

## 2. Evidence in depth

### (a) The founding mechanism

Two Hazen-group papers founded the field. A 2011 Nature metabolomics screen identified choline, TMAO and betaine as gut-flora-dependent phosphatidylcholine metabolites predicting CVD risk; in mice, dietary supplementation "promoted up-regulation of multiple macrophage scavenger receptors linked to atherosclerosis" and promoted atherosclerosis, while "suppression of intestinal microflora in atherosclerosis-prone mice inhibited dietary choline-enhanced atherosclerosis" (Gut flora metabolism of phosphatidylcholine promotes cardiovascular disease — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3086762/, accessed 2026-11-15). The 2013 Nature Medicine carnitine paper did the same for L-carnitine: its oral challenge (250 mg d3-carnitine after a 12-hour fast, alone or with an 8-oz steak ≈180 mg carnitine) produced significantly more TMAO in omnivores than vegans/vegetarians "through a microbiota-dependent mechanism"; in mice, chronic carnitine feeding "markedly enhanced synthesis of TMA/TMAO, and increased atherosclerosis, but not following suppression of intestinal microbiota," roughly doubling aortic-root disease burden; and in 2,595 cardiac-evaluation subjects, plasma L-carnitine predicted incident MACE "but only among subjects with concurrently high TMAO levels" (Intestinal microbiota metabolism of L-carnitine... — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3650111/, accessed 2026-11-15).

The 2019 JCI follow-up mapped the full human pathway — L-carnitine → γ-butyrobetaine (γBB) → TMA → TMAO: omnivores showed ">20-fold" more labelled-TMAO generation than vegans/vegetarians despite similar fasting carnitine and γBB; the microbial γBB→TMA step (Emergencia timonensis) "is induced by omnivorous dietary patterns and chronic l-carnitine exposure"; and an additional month of daily supplementation produced "no further increase... in omnivores, but continued to increase d3-TMAO generation in vegans/vegetarians" (l-Carnitine in omnivorous diets induces an atherogenic gut microbial pathway in humans — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6307959/, accessed 2026-11-15). Individual variation is wide: omnivores carry 10-fold higher odds of the "high TMAO-producer phenotype" than vegetarians on oral carnitine challenge testing (Identification of TMAO-producer phenotype... — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6691853/, accessed 2026-11-15), and a microbiome classifier predicts producer status with AUROC 0.81–0.80 (Characterization of TMAO productivity from carnitine challenge... — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7676756/, accessed 2026-11-15). At supplement doses — 1–4 g/day for 12 or 24 weeks across 11 trials in healthy subjects — L-carnitine "induce[s] an increase of fasting plasma trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) levels," without inflammatory or oxidative-stress changes (The bright and the dark sides of L-carnitine supplementation: a systematic review — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32958033/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### (b) TMAO and the kidney

TMAO is renally cleared and accumulates as filtration falls: "Plasma TMAO is cleared by the kidney, and TMAO levels are elevated in CKD" (The role of trimethylamine N-oxide as a mediator of cardiovascular complications in chronic kidney disease — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28807612/, accessed 2026-11-15). In CKD 3–5, GFR was "the dominant variable affecting TMAO"; dialysis does not lower TMAO, while transplantation reduces levels "to that of controls" (Serum Trimethylamine-N-Oxide Is Strongly Related to Renal Function and Predicts Outcome in Chronic Kidney Disease — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26751065/, accessed 2026-11-15). Magnitudes: dialysis patients median 94.4 μM versus 3.3 μM in controls, 71.2 μM pre- versus 11.4 μM post-transplant; TMAO independently predicted coronary burden and mortality in CKD (Serum Trimethylamine-N-Oxide is Elevated in CKD and Correlates with Coronary Atherosclerosis Burden — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26229137/, accessed 2026-11-15).

Outcomes: among 521 stable CKD subjects, top-quartile TMAO carried "a 2.8-fold increased mortality risk," remaining predictive after adjustment (HR 1.93) (Gut microbiota-dependent TMAO pathway contributes to both development of renal insufficiency and mortality... — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4312512/, accessed 2026-11-15). In 10,564 community adults, higher serial TMAO predicted incident CKD (quintile HRs up to 2.24) and faster annualized eGFR decline "with monotonic dose–response relationships" (The Gut Microbial Metabolite Trimethylamine N-oxide, Incident CKD, and Kidney Function Decline — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38593157/, accessed 2026-11-15). Mechanistically, "elevated dietary choline or TMAO directly led to progressive renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis and dysfunction" in mice (Circulation Research source above), and in human renal fibroblasts TMAO promoted activation, proliferation and collagen production "via the PERK/Akt/mTOR pathway, NLRP3, and caspase-1 signaling" (The Fibrotic Effects of TMAO on Human Renal Fibroblasts... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34769294/, accessed 2026-11-15). A dietary-source review adds that preformed fish TMAO (variable by depth, processing, storage) is a distinct exposure route, and that "the evidence in CKD patients has not been consistent" (The dietary source of trimethylamine N-oxide and clinical outcomes: an unexpected liaison — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37915930/, accessed 2026-11-15). The instructive null: in 1,243 EVOLVE hemodialysis patients — ~80% ≥56 μM, maximum 1,103 μM — "We found no evidence linking TMAO to adverse clinical outcomes" (Trimethylamine N-Oxide and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients with ESKD Receiving Maintenance Hemodialysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30665924/, accessed 2026-11-15): at the highest TMAO concentrations on record, the association disappears.

### (c) Cardiovascular associations — and the causality debate

The association literature is strong on its own terms: fasting TMAO predicted incident MACE over 3 years in 4,007 angiography patients, HR 2.54 top versus bottom quartile (NEJM source above); a dose-response meta-analysis of 17 studies (26,167 subjects) found high TMAO associated with all-cause mortality (HR 1.91; I² = 94%) and MACCE (HR 1.67), risk rising 7.6% per 10 μmol/L (Gut microbe-generated metabolite trimethylamine-N-oxide as cardiovascular risk biomarker... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29020409/, accessed 2026-11-15); and in MESA (6,767 adults free of ASCVD at baseline, median 11.3-year follow-up, adjustment including antibiotic use and diet) incident ASCVD rose dose-dependently (top-quintile HR 1.33, P-trend = 0.01), and risk "appeared possibly larger... among individuals with lower baseline renal function," though not significantly (TMAO and risk of incident cardiovascular events in MESA — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40603925/, accessed 2026-11-15).

Counter-evidence, equally specific:

- **Genetics.** In bidirectional Mendelian randomization, "genetically predicted higher TMAO and carnitine were not associated with higher odds of T2DM, AF, CAD, MI, stroke, and CKD after Bonferroni correction"; diabetes and CKD instead causally raise TMAO, and the authors conclude that "observational evidence for cardiovascular diseases may be due to confounding or reverse causality" (Assessment of Causal Direction... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31167879/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- **A direct mouse contradiction.** In ApoE−/−CETP mice chronically fed high-dose L-carnitine, TMAO "inversely correlated with aortic lesion size," high TMAO correlating with smaller lesions; the authors suggest a protective effect in this model (L-Carnitine intake and high trimethylamine N-oxide plasma levels correlate with low aortic lesions in ApoE(-/-) transgenic mice expressing CETP — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26584136/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- **The fish problem.** Fish meals transiently raise TMAO: next-morning likelihood of exceeding the 6.4 μM MACE-associated cut-off was 45% after fish versus 20% after red meat, with return to baseline within one day (Circulating trimethylamine N-oxide levels following fish or seafood consumption — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9283263/, accessed 2026-11-15). Two RCTs showed "healthy" diets (marine omega-3, whole-grain) significantly *increased* plasma TMAO, which tracked fish and whole-grain — but not meat — intake; the authors conclude TMAO "is not a universally valid biomarker of cardiometabolic risk independent of the background diet" (Plasma TMAO increase after healthy diets... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34091663/, accessed 2026-11-15). A review notes animal studies reporting "neutral or even beneficial effects of TMAO or its precursors" and cardioprotective seafood-rich diets that directly supply TMAO (Trimethylamine N-Oxide in Relation to Cardiometabolic Health-Cause or Effect? — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32392758/, accessed 2026-11-15); on the animal side, fish protein raised TMAO to 7.03 ± 2.83 μmol/L (vs ~1 for casein/soy) and doubled plaque area in apoE-null mice (Fish protein increases circulating levels of trimethylamine-N-oxide and accelerates aortic lesion formation in apoE null mice — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26502377/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- **The carnitine carotid RCT.** In 157 metabolic-syndrome participants given 2 g/day L-carnitine for 6 months, "no difference in TPV change between arms was found," but the carnitine group had "a greater increase in carotid atherosclerotic stenosis of 9.3% (p = 0.02)" plus higher LDL-C (Progression of atherosclerosis with carnitine supplementation: a randomized controlled trial in the metabolic syndrome — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35366920/, accessed 2026-11-15) — concerning, but in MetS patients with a null primary endpoint.

## 3. Human evidence (what is directly measured in people)

- **Challenge/isotope studies:** omnivore-versus-vegan divergence and pathway induction on ≥2-month supplementation (Koeth 2013; JCI 2019; Gut 2019); antibiotic suppression and re-emergence (NEJM 2013).
- **Supplementation trials:** 1–4 g/day for 12–24 weeks raises fasting TMAO in healthy subjects without inflammatory or oxidative-stress changes (JISSN 2020, 11 studies); the only atherosclerosis-outcome trial (2 g/day × 6 months, MetS) is detailed in §2c.
- **Cohorts with outcomes:** 2,595 cardiac evaluation (Nat Med 2013); 4,007 angiography (NEJM 2013); 521 CKD mortality (Circ Res 2015); 220 CKD coronary burden (JASN 2016); 10,564 incident CKD (JASN 2024); 6,767 MESA ASCVD (Sci Rep 2025); 1,243 ESKD hemodialysis — null (CJASN 2019).
- **Training and exercise:** in sedentary (n = 17) versus endurance-trained (n = 7) males, "Neither the HFC nor the HFD evoked a detectable change in plasma TMAO," with no fasting or postprandial differences "despite the endurance-trained group consuming more dietary precursors" (Fasting and postprandial trimethylamine N-oxide in sedentary and endurance-trained males... — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8371342/, accessed 2026-11-15); a 2026 review finds TMAO does "not consistently correlate with aerobic fitness, habitual physical activity, or training status," with exercise-only interventions rarely changing it (Trimethylamine N-oxide in exercise physiology... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42287393/, accessed 2026-11-15).

The pattern: metabolic exposure is real and reproducible; adverse-outcome associations concentrate in older, higher-risk, kidney-disease populations. Short-form references carry full URLs in §2.

## 4. What is NOT established

- **Causality in humans.** Every counterweight in §2c — the MR nulls with reverse causation, the ESKD null, the inverse carnitine-mouse lesions, the fish dissociation — leaves the TMAO-causes-disease direction unproven.
- **Renal harm from oral carnitine at supplement doses in healthy people: not found.** The renal-safety review states "The risks and benefits of L-carnitine on the athletes' and bodybuilders' kidney have not been evaluated yet," while L-carnitine "up to 6000 mg/day is generally considered to be a safe supplement at least in healthy adults" (The Renal Safety of L-Carnitine, L-Arginine, and Glutamine in Athletes and Bodybuilders — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30341034/, accessed 2026-11-15). No saved source shows kidney injury from oral carnitine in healthy people.
- **The chronic fasting-TMAO increment at 2–3 g/day is not quantified** in any saved source: the systematic review reports a rise without magnitudes, and challenge-test fold-changes are acute labelled-TMAO areas, not fasting baselines on chronic dosing.
- **Trained-population data are thin:** one small null trial (7 trained subjects) and a narrative review; no longitudinal training study of TMAO production or renal clearance exists, and cycling versus continuous dosing has never been studied.
- **No outcome data exist for transient postprandial excursions** (fish meals), nor for supplement-driven chronic elevation in healthy people.
- **Microbiome "fixes" are unproven for TMAO:** a randomized vegan-donor FMT trial shifted composition without functional effect on TMAO production (Effect of Vegan Fecal Microbiota Transplantation... — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29581220/, accessed 2026-11-15); fiber/polyphenol/probiotic modulation is proposed but untested against TMAO endpoints (Revisiting the Role of Carnitine in Heart Disease Through the Lens of the Gut Microbiota — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39683637/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- **Dose framing.** The formal risk assessment sets the observed safe level at 2000 mg/day L-carnitine equivalents for chronic supplementation, noting "the data for intakes above 2000mg/day are not sufficient for a confident conclusion of long-term safety," even though "much higher levels have been tested without adverse effects" (Risk assessment for carnitine — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16901595/, accessed 2026-11-15) — 2–3 g/day thus sits above the formally documented level, within the "generally considered safe" range.

## 6. Open questions for the parent

- **Personal baseline.** The producer phenotype varies and is inducible. If the client plans chronic supplementation, a practical check is fasting plasma TMAO at baseline and after 8–12 weeks on the intended dose; no outcome trial justifies intervening on the number itself. Any draw must be fish-free for 24–48 hours (45% of fish-eaters exceeded the 6.4 μM cut-off the next morning).
- **Dose framing.** 2–3 g/day sits above the 2006 observed-safe-level of 2,000 mg/day (data above it explicitly insufficient for confident long-term conclusions) yet below the "generally considered safe" ceiling of 6,000 mg/day — state both.
- **Population mismatch.** Adverse-outcome associations live in cardiac-catheterization, CKD, and MetS cohorts. Nothing in the saved literature documents harm in healthy, normal-kidney athletes — say so as a boundary of the evidence, not as exoneration.
- **Cross-stream link to Brief 03.** The kidney is both TMAO's excretion route and the paper's organ of concern. No evidence ties transient exertional eGFR dips to harmful TMAO retention, but chronic supplementation in a hiker with established CKD is where the TMAO literature raises real caution — route contraindication language through this stream.
- **Suggested stance.** Established: microbiome-dependent production, omnivore/vegan divergence, fasting-TMAO rise at supplement doses. Association-only: CVD and CKD outcomes. Not established/absent: harm in healthy supplement users — including any renal injury — at supplement doses in people with normal kidney function.
