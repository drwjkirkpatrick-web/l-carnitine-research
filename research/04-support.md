# 04 — Carnitine as renal support under stress (CORE evidence stream)

Dossier for Brief 04. Every factual claim carries a footnote to a source whose full fetched text is saved under `research/support/pages/`; verbatim supporting quotes and structured claims are in `research/findings_support.json`.

## 1 Overview

The question for this stream: is there direct evidence that exogenous L-carnitine supports the kidney under stress? The evidence sorts into three tiers.

**Tier 1 — regulatory precedent.** Injectable levocarnitine is FDA-approved specifically for the carnitine deficiency of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients on dialysis[^1] — the one setting in which renal medicine officially prescribes carnitine because the failing kidney and the dialyzer together deplete it.

**Tier 2 — mechanism and preclinical protection.** The proximal tubule runs predominantly on mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation (FAO), a pathway carnitine gates; ischemia inhibits FAO, causing acyl-CoA/acylcarnitine accumulation and ATP collapse, and carnitine-side interventions blunt this in rodents and tubule cells.

**Tier 3 — clinical stress states.** Small randomized trials exist in hemodialysis complications, contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN), septic shock, transplant delayed graft function and valproate toxicity — with mixed results.

Bottom line: carnitine replacement when the kidney is failing is established medicine; carnitine as *prophylaxis* for a stressed but working kidney — the thru-hiker's situation — is biologically plausible but unproven, and the trial-level record points to nulls as often as to benefits.

## 2 Evidence in depth

### 2.1 The dialysis precedent (scope a)

The US label for levocarnitine injection lists, among its indications, "For the prevention and treatment of carnitine deficiency in patients with end stage renal disease who are undergoing dialysis."[^1] with a recommended starting dose of 10–20 mg/kg dry body weight given as a slow bolus into the venous return line after each dialysis session.[^1] The label's pharmacology text explains why: ESRD patients "may have low plasma carnitine concentrations and an increased ratio of acylcarnitine/carnitine because of reduced intake of meat and dairy products, reduced renal synthesis and dialytic losses."[^1]

Among 150 maintenance-dialysis patients, 25.3% met free-carnitine deficiency (serum free carnitine below 20 μmol/L) and 86.7% met acyl/free-ratio insufficiency (ratio above 0.4); longer dialysis vintage predicted lower free carnitine, and a single session stripped 64±4% of serum free carnitine on hemodialysis and 75±7% on hemodiafiltration.[^2] Pharmacokinetic modeling reaches the same conclusion: each session causes rapid plasma reductions with re-equilibration from muscle between sessions, and over time "the ongoing dialytic loss of L-carnitine may lead to a slow depletion of the compound, contributing to a possible secondary deficiency."[^3]

The kidney's role is bidirectional: the healthy kidney conserves carnitine through "extensive and adaptive tubular reabsorption", so kidney disease itself disturbs carnitine homeostasis before any dialyzer is attached;[^4] the classic pharmacokinetic review states the endogenous pool is maintained by diet, biosynthesis and "extensive renal tubular reabsorption from glomerular filtrate."[^5] The same 2003 pharmacology review notes that intravenous L-carnitine is approved for secondary deficiency caused by long-term hemodialysis.[^4]

Trial evidence in dialysis patients is mixed but instructive. A multicenter randomized controlled trial found levocarnitine reduced left ventricular mass index, lowered NT-proBNP and improved the erythropoietin responsiveness index in hemodialysis patients with left ventricular hypertrophy.[^6] A 2026 randomized, double-blind pilot in ESA-hyporesponsive hemodialysis patients cut the erythropoietin resistance index by 25% versus 3% with placebo.[^7] A 2014 systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs found l-carnitine significantly decreased serum LDL and CRP but did not confirm earlier findings of benefits for hemoglobin or erythropoietin dose.[^8] And the 2022 Cochrane review concluded: "The available evidence does not currently support the use of carnitine supplementation in the treatment of dialysis-related carnitine deficiency", while allowing possible slight anemia-marker improvement and stressing limited data.[^9]

Safety caveats sit on the same label: oral levocarnitine "has not been evaluated in patients with renal insufficiency", and chronic high oral doses in ESRD can accumulate trimethylamine (TMA) and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) "since these metabolites are normally excreted in the urine."[^1]

### 2.2 Why the ischemic tubule needs carnitine (scope b)

The rationale begins with tubule energetics: "proximal tubules use fatty acid oxidation, which generates more adenosine triphosphate (ATP) than glucose metabolism, as its preferred metabolic pathway" to power reabsorption.[^10] A 2025 review frames AKI as an energy-metabolism disease in which tubular cells "are highly metabolically active" and their acylcarnitines "are crucial in transporting fatty acids from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation".[^11]

Ischemia attacks exactly this pathway. In the seminal formulation, during ischemic acute renal failure long-chain fatty-acid metabolites accumulate as β-oxidation is inhibited, and "lysophospholipids, long-chain acyl carnitines, and long-chain acyl coenzyme A inhibit proximal tubule Na+K(+)-ATPase."[^12]

Carnitine-side interventions protect in models. L-carnitine pretreatment (100 mg/kg intraperitoneally) lowered renal tissue malondialdehyde, myeloperoxidase activity and nitrite/nitrate after rat renal ischemia-reperfusion, and the authors concluded "carnitine protects kidney tissue against ischaemia-reperfusion injury."[^13] In human HK-2 proximal tubule cells, L-carnitine blocked hydrogen-peroxide-induced injury through "the inhibition of oxidative damage, mitochondria dysfunction and ultimately inhibition of cell apoptosis".[^14] Stimulating carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1), the enzyme that imports long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, with C75 "recovered ATP depletion, improved renal function, attenuated tissue injury" after renal ischemia-reperfusion in rats[^15] — pharmacologic evidence that the carnitine-gated pathway is the injury-relevant one. Carnitine partially prevented renal damage in a rat infrarenal aortic ischemia-reperfusion model,[^16] and in a 2026 rat study, L-carnitine (200 mg/kg for 10 days) attenuated oxidative, inflammatory and apoptotic pathways in urographin-induced contrast nephrotoxicity.[^17]

### 2.3 The kidney as a β-oxidation organ (scope d)

The same energetics carry into chronic disease: in diabetic kidney disease, impaired carnitine-dependent FAO tracks ectopic lipid accumulation and worse kidney function; l-carnitine ameliorated injury in the rat model and, in a single-center randomized trial of peritoneal-dialysis patients, "l-carnitine supplementation preserved residual renal function and increased urine volume," with urine volume correlating with tubular-injury improvement.[^18]

## 3 Human evidence under stress (scopes c, e, f)

**Contrast-induced nephropathy.** Two small clinical studies: in CKD patients receiving radiocontrast, carnitine pretreatment "prevented the increase in urinary NGAL and reduced SCr below basal levels"[^19]; in 202 patients undergoing elective percutaneous coronary intervention, oral L-carnitine prevented the post-contrast rise in plasma NGAL at 12 hours.[^20] Both rest on surrogate endpoints; no large CIN outcome trial of carnitine was located in this sweep.

**Septic shock and critical illness.** A pilot randomized trial (31 enrolled) found L-carnitine infusion safe in vasopressor-dependent septic shock, with 28-day mortality lower in the treatment arm (4/16 vs 9/15, P = .048).[^21] The 250-patient phase-2 RACE adaptive randomized trial then found the best levocarnitine dose (18 g) "did not meaningfully reduce cumulative organ failure at 48 hours".[^22] and a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis concluded there is "low-quality evidence that the use of L-carnitine has no significant effect on reducing 28-day mortality in septic shock patients."[^23]

**Transplant ischemia-reperfusion.** A pilot randomized trial of oral L-carnitine around deceased-donor kidney transplantation did not reduce delayed graft function, although 3-month graft loss was lower in the supplemented group (1 vs 6 patients, P = .05).[^24]

**Valproate mitochondrial toxicity.** A 2026 systematic review of L-carnitine and acetyl-L-carnitine in acute poisonings concluded L-carnitine "may be taken into consideration in cases of severe valproate toxicity with hyperammonemia or hepatotoxicity," within thorough resuscitation protocols, while noting a retrospective valproate cohort showing no kinetic or survival advantage.[^25]

**Stress-state markers (e).** In the septic-shock metabolomics substudy, L-carnitine treatment increased all measured acylcarnitines, an effect sustained at least 36 hours after the infusion ended and "more prominent among nonsurvivors", who already had higher baseline acylcarnitines[^26] — the acyl/free-carnitine pattern reads as a marker of incomplete β-oxidation under inflammatory stress. Kidney function governs carnitine economy in shock: kidney-function estimates leveraging cystatin C provided the best pharmacokinetic model fit for high-dose L-carnitine.[^27] On the transporter side, pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β and IFNγ downregulate OCTN2 (SLC22A5) and reduce cellular l-carnitine through PPARγ/RXRα pathways in FHC colonocytes[^28] — in-vitro evidence that inflammation can throttle carnitine transport — and in septic AKI, SLC22A5 emerged as a macrophage immunometabolic target in mice, where targeting it improved survival and deterred septic AKI.[^29]

**Losses under exertion (f).** Human data are old and thin. Five-day fasting raised urinary carnitine excretion 4.6-fold in young men and heavy running raised it slightly.[^30] In endurance athletes, six months of training lowered muscle free and total carnitine and was accompanied by significantly increased post-exercise urinary excretion of esterified and total carnitine — changes prevented by oral L-carnitine administration.[^31] In Alaskan sled dogs during multiday submaximal exercise, acylcarnitine profiles shifted chain-specifically — long-chain acylcarnitines rose steadily, acetylcarnitine (C2) rose early then fell in the later phase — consistent with accelerated acyl-group export under sustained exertion.[^32]

## 4 What is NOT established (gaps, failed hints)

- **Carnitine losses in sweat — NOT FOUND.** No primary study measuring carnitine in human sweat was located (PubMed queries: carnitine sweat loss; sweat carnitine). Any trail-clinic claim that sweating depletes carnitine is unsupported.
- **Carnitine status under dehydration or water restriction — NOT FOUND.** No study isolates water restriction or dehydration effects on carnitine economy (PubMed query: carnitine dehydration water restriction returned nothing relevant).
- **Direct human evidence that renal OCTN2-mediated reabsorption is impaired in sepsis or critical illness — NOT FOUND.** The OCTN2-inflammation link is established in colonocytes in vitro[^28] and in septic-AKI mouse bioinformatics[^29]; no human study demonstrates reduced renal carnitine reabsorption during systemic inflammation.
- **No carnitine randomized trial with a hard renal primary endpoint was located.** Dialysis trials target anemia and cardiac surrogates; sepsis trials target SOFA and mortality; CIN studies target NGAL and creatinine. An AKI-incidence trial of carnitine does not exist in this sweep.
- **The negative and cautious findings are part of the record:** Cochrane does not support routine dialysis supplementation[^9]; the sepsis meta-analysis is null on mortality[^23]; the DGF trial was null on its primary endpoint[^24]; a valproate retrospective cohort showed no kinetic or survival advantage[^25]; and the label warns of TMA/TMAO accumulation with chronic high oral doses in renal failure.[^1]

## 6 Open questions for the parent

1. Dose translation: the dialysis IV dose (10–20 mg/kg post-session) is a replacement dose for a depleted, dialyzed patient; the oral label range is 1–3 g/day for a 50-kg adult.[^1] What, if any, oral dose is defensible for exertion-stressed but non-azotemic hikers is not answered in this literature.
2. The acyl/free ratio (>0.4 as dialysis insufficiency[^2]) is the cleanest lab marker found here; is it orderable outside research settings, and worth proposing in the trail-clinic chapter?
3. The TMAO question belongs to Brief 05, but the label's TMA/TMAO accumulation warning in ESRD[^1] must temper any high-dose oral recommendation for anyone with renal impairment.
4. Sepsis trials used 6–18 g IV — an order of magnitude above supplement doses. Read the null RACE result narrowly: megadose rescue in established shock did not reverse organ failure, not that carnitine is useless.
5. OCTN2 regulation under exertion (as opposed to inflammation) is unstudied; if transport is cytokine-sensitive, exertion inflammation could modestly raise carnitine requirements — hypothesis-level only.
6. The strongest human renal signal in a non-dialysis setting is the peritoneal-dialysis RCT's preserved residual renal function[^18] — worth its own paragraph in the paper.
