# Brief 03 — Renal Stress of Desert Thru-Hiking: What the Stressor Side Does to Kidneys

Research dossier (Brief 03) for the L-carnitine grounded review. Every claim below is grounded in saved page text under `research/renalstress/pages/`; every quote in `research/findings_renalstress.json` is a verbatim substring of its saved page. Access date for all sources: 2026-11-15.

## 1. Overview

A desert thru-hike repeats, day after day for weeks, the triad the renal literature identifies as the kidney's highest-risk combination: prolonged exertion, heat stress, and dehydration — frequently compounded by NSAID use and, in some hikers, by the opposite error of overhydration. Exercise acutely reduces renal plasma flow and triggers hormone-mediated fluid retention; these adaptations are protective in moderation but "become maladaptive with dehydration, heat stress, or excessive fluid intake" (Exercise and Kidney Health: Core Curriculum 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41389078/, accessed 2026-11-15). Hyperthermia itself diverts blood flow toward the skin and raises renal energetic demand, creating a "localized supply-demand mismatch" that can leave kidney tissue hypoxic (The kidney under heat stress: a vulnerable state — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39688252/, accessed 2026-11-15). The evidence below comes from the closest available analogues to thru-hiking.

## 2. Evidence in depth (by stressor)

### (a) Exercise-associated acute kidney injury (EAKI)

- Marathon: in a prospective study of 22 Hartford Marathon runners, 82% developed a creatinine increase meeting AKIN stage 1–2 criteria, 73% showed urinary sediment diagnostic of tubular injury, and injury/repair biomarkers (IL-18, KIM-1, NGAL, YKL-40, MCP-1) peaked the day after the race (Kidney Injury and Repair Biomarkers in Marathon Runners — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28363731/, accessed 2026-11-15). A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found the largest post-marathon rises in TIMP-2*IGFBP7, copeptin and L-FABP, with heterogeneous 24-hour recovery patterns (Acute Kidney Injury Biomarkers in Marathon Runners: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41155766/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- 161-km ultramarathon: among 627 Western States finishes, 36.2% met RIFLE "risk" or "injury" criteria, and repeat-race analysis found no evidence that prior AKI produced worse subsequent renal dysfunction (Does Acute Kidney Injury From an Ultramarathon Increase the Risk for Greater Subsequent Injury? — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26657822/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Multistage desert ultras — the closest thru-hike analogue: across 250-km, 6-stage RacingThePlanet races (Jordan, Atacama, Gobi), cumulative AKI incidence was 41.4%, with 43.8–48.4% of runners at "risk" and 18.8–32% at "injury" grade across stages (Incidence and Prevalence of Acute Kidney Injury During Multistage Ultramarathons — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513390/, accessed 2026-11-15). The BIERS cohort (Gobi, Sahara, Namibia) found 55–80% of participants incurring AKI yet "no apparent cumulative effect," with resolution of renal function to near baseline between stages (A prospective cohort study of acute kidney injury in multi-stage ultramarathon runners: the Biochemistry in Endurance Runner Study (BIERS) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24650338/, accessed 2026-11-15). This daily stress-and-resolution cycle is the physiologic signature a thru-hike repeats for weeks.
- Counterpoint: in 24 finishers of a 120-km ultra who were properly hydrated and used no NSAIDs, AKI prevalence by GFR-based criteria was 0–12.5%, and the authors asked whether biomarker rises reflect structural injury or metabolic adaptation (Ultramarathon and Renal Function: Does Exercise-Induced Acute Kidney Injury Really Exist in Common Conditions? — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344994/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### (b) Heat and water limitation

- Heat amplifies exercise's renal risk: the kidneys "are at increased risk of pathological events during heat stress... compounded by dehydration and exercise," and heat-stress AKI is believed to contribute to occupational CKD epidemics affecting "upwards of 45 million individuals in the global workforce" (Kidney physiology and pathophysiology during heat stress — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33997113/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Field data: 189 sugarcane cutters working 34–42 °C days showed significant cross-shift increases in serum creatinine, uric acid and urea nitrogen, increased urine concentration, and pre-shift eGFR <60 mL/min in 14% of male workers (Heat stress, dehydration, and kidney function in sugarcane cutters in El Salvador — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26209462/, accessed 2026-11-15). The heat-stress-nephropathy literature proposes recurrent exertion-heat dehydration with uric-acid crystalluria as the mechanism and "increased hydration with water and salt" as a candidate prevention (Heat Stress Nephropathy From Exercise-Induced Uric Acid Crystalluria — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26455995/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Hormonal signature: in volunteers dehydrated by 3% body mass before 60 minutes of treadmill walking at 35 °C, plasma renin, aldosterone, arginine vasopressin and catecholamines rose while creatinine clearance and urine flow fell; rehydration blunted the vasopressin and catecholamine responses (Effects of hydration state on hormonal and renal responses during moderate exercise in the heat — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9349646/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Military: among young service members hospitalized for exertional heat illness, AKI was the most common end-organ damage at 73–86%, ahead of liver injury (Acute kidney injury and liver damage are the most common forms of end organ damage in exertional heat illness — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694931/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### (c) Exertional rhabdomyolysis

- Thru-hike-specific mechanism: prolonged *downhill* walking is eccentric. Forty minutes of downhill walking at −28% grade with a 10% body-mass load produced ~19% quadriceps strength loss, CK elevation and severe soreness; a small prior bout of non-damaging downhill walking attenuated the damage by 47–64% (Prevention of downhill walking-induced muscle damage by non-damaging downhill walking — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28288187/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Incidence and thresholds: a systematic review of endurance events (n=813) found 43.5% of participants met exertional rhabdomyolysis criteria (CK > 5000 U/L), 16.4% had rhabdomyolysis with AKI — 96.92% of them ultra-endurance runners — with biomarkers usually back to baseline within ~6 days (Exertional rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury in endurance sports: A systematic review — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202487/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- CK alone is a poor predictor: in 499 military recruits, mean CK rose from 223 to 1226 IU/L over two weeks of basic training with no clinical rhabdomyolysis, and CK >50× the upper limit of normal was suggested as more specific (Serum creatine kinase after exercise: drawing the line between physiological response and exertional rhabdomyolysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334169/, accessed 2026-11-15); in 200 hospitalized exertional rhabdomyolysis patients only 8.5% developed AKI and "there was no association between serum CK levels and the risk of AKI" (Acute Kidney Injury in Hospitalized Patients With Exertional Rhabdomyolysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39136944/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Desert hiking case: a 41-year-old Grand Canyon hiker developed concomitant exercise-associated hyponatremic encephalopathy (Na 114 mmol/L), exertional rhabdomyolysis, bilateral compartment syndromes and AKI after overconsuming water during a hot descent, and was evacuated by helicopter mid-seizure (Exercise-Associated Hyponatremic Encephalopathy, Rhabdomyolysis, and Acute Kidney Injury While Hiking in the Grand Canyon — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39444363/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### (d) NSAID culture on trail

- Prevalence: 53% of 167 surveyed ultra-endurance runners reported NSAID use (ibuprofen most common), persisting despite 77% awareness of harms and "entrenched norms around pain management" (The prevalence and perceptions of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use among ultra-endurance runners — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40947642/, accessed 2026-11-15); 30% of Ironman triathletes used NSAIDs (NSAID use increases the risk of developing hyponatremia during an Ironman triathlon — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16679974/, accessed 2026-11-15); and the desert-ultra RCT literature notes that up to 75% of ultramarathon runners ingest NSAIDs during competition (Ibuprofen versus placebo effect on acute kidney injury in ultramarathons: a randomised controlled trial — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28679502/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Hikers specifically: a survey of Appalachian Trail long-distance hikers found analgesics carried by 72.5% (72.1% of carriers used them) and that "the majority of the analgesic medications were nonsteroidal, anti-inflammatory agents"; 82% of hikers reported injuries or illnesses costing a mean 4.7 trail days (Health Care Needs of Appalachian Trail Hikers — https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/jfp-archived-issues/1993-volume_36-37/JFP_1993-05_v36_i5_health-care-needs-of-appalachian-trail-h.pdf, accessed 2026-11-15). The "vitamin I" nickname itself could not be verified in the indexed literature (see §4).
- NSAID + dehydration as an AKI setup: in the randomized trial across desert 50-mile races, 52% of ibuprofen users developed AKI versus 34% of placebo (number needed to harm 5.5), with greater race weight loss raising AKI odds (Ibuprofen versus placebo effect on acute kidney injury in ultramarathons — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28679502/, accessed 2026-11-15). In hospitalized exertional rhabdomyolysis, preadmission NSAID use (64.7% of AKI cases vs 21.9% without AKI) and dehydration were the significant cofactors; eliminating both "could reduce the risk of potential AKI... by 92.6%" (Acute Kidney Injury in Hospitalized Patients With Exertional Rhabdomyolysis — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39136944/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Null trials to weigh honestly: a single 600-mg ibuprofen dose did not add renal stress to 5.7 h of endurance cycling at 33 °C (Renal stress and kidney injury biomarkers in response to endurance cycling in the heat with and without ibuprofen — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29784554/, accessed 2026-11-15) and did not exacerbate AKI markers after 1 h of running at 35 °C (Effect of Ibuprofen on Markers of Acute Kidney Injury, Intestinal Injury, and Endotoxemia after Running in the Heat — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39876077/, accessed 2026-11-15). Dose, duration, and hydration state plausibly explain the divergence from the repeated-dose desert RCT.

### (e) Exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH) — the opposite failure mode

- 13% of 488 studied Boston Marathon finishers were hyponatremic and 0.6% critically so; multivariate risk factors were postrace weight gain (OR 4.2) and finishing time >4 h (OR 7.4) — overdrinking, not underdrinking, drives EAH (Hyponatremia among runners in the Boston Marathon — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15829535/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- EAH is defined as sodium <135 mmol/L during or up to 24 h after prolonged exercise and "most commonly has overlapping signs and symptoms with heat exhaustion and exertional heat stroke," making field misdiagnosis a recognized cause of worsened outcomes (Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia: 2019 Update — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32044213/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Desert hikers: among heat-ill Grand Canyon hikers, 7 of 44 analyzed patients had symptomatic hyponatremia <130 mmol/L (three with grand mal seizures), with indirect evidence they were hyperhydrated (Exertional heat illness and hyponatremia in hikers — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10530529/, accessed 2026-11-15); suspected hyponatremia comprised 19% of clinical assessments in the park's 474-case HRI series, 90% of them hikers (Exertional heat-related illnesses at the Grand Canyon National Park, 2004-2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24119571/, accessed 2026-11-15).

### (f) Baseline protections (the comparator for any adjunct)

- The current standard named by the 2026 AJKD core curriculum: "individualized hydration plans, electrolyte replacement, and avoidance of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs" (Exercise and Kidney Health: Core Curriculum 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41389078/, accessed 2026-11-15), with water-plus-salt hydration specifically proposed against heat stress nephropathy (Heat Stress Nephropathy From Exercise-Induced Uric Acid Crystalluria — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26455995/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Heat acclimatization: 23 days of progressive acclimatisation reduced post-exercise creatinine and AKI incidence during standardized hot exercise, with AKI most frequent in the unacclimatised state (Variation in renal responses to exercise in the heat with progressive acclimatisation — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31085130/, accessed 2026-11-15); but 10 days of heat acclimation blunted the core-temperature rise without reducing creatinine/NGAL increases, hinting the acclimated kidney may be more thermally sensitive (Heat acclimation alters the relation between hyperthermia and biomarkers of kidney function during exercise heat stress — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41247794/, accessed 2026-11-15). Acclimatization helps but is not a complete renal shield.
- Field guidance: the WMS heat illness guidelines (Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Heat Illness: 2024 Update — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38425235/, accessed 2026-11-15) and the WMS EAH guidelines (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32044213/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Against eccentric damage, a small preconditioning bout of downhill walking attenuates later loaded-downhill damage by 47–64% (Prevention of downhill walking-induced muscle damage by non-damaging downhill walking — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28288187/, accessed 2026-11-15) — the trail equivalent is starting with modest daily mileages and gentle grades.

## 3. Human evidence

Ranked by proximity to thru-hiking: (i) multistage desert ultramarathoners — stage AKI prevalence 63–78%, resolving between stages (Incidence and Prevalence of Acute Kidney Injury During Multistage Ultramarathons — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513390/; BIERS cohort — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24650338/, accessed 2026-11-15); (ii) the desert-race ibuprofen RCT (Ibuprofen versus placebo effect on acute kidney injury in ultramarathons — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28679502/, accessed 2026-11-15); (iii) Grand Canyon hiker EMS/clinic series (Exertional heat-related illnesses at the Grand Canyon National Park, 2004-2009 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24119571/; Exertional heat illness and hyponatremia in hikers — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10530529/, accessed 2026-11-15); (iv) military heat casualties and recruits (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42694931/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334169/, accessed 2026-11-15); (v) occupational heat workers (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26209462/, accessed 2026-11-15); and (vi) marathon/ultra cohorts (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28363731/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26657822/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344994/, accessed 2026-11-15). Thru-hiker surveys document morbidity and medication use but no renal-function measurements (The Epidemiology and Medical Morbidity of Long-Distance Backpackers on the John Muir Trail in the Sierra Nevada — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29804621/, accessed 2026-11-15).

## 4. What is NOT established

- Whether endurance-event biomarker elevations represent true structural injury or functional adaptation is explicitly unresolved (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33344994/, accessed 2026-11-15); tubular-injury evidence (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28363731/, accessed 2026-11-15) and complete inter-race/inter-stage recovery (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26657822/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24650338/, accessed 2026-11-15) coexist.
- No renal-function study of an actual multiweek thru-hike exists in the indexed literature; all kidney inferences are by analogy (multistage racing, occupational heat, military heat illness, park EMS series).
- The "vitamin I" nickname for ibuprofen could not be verified in any indexed source; NSAID *use* by hikers and endurance athletes is documented (see §2d), but the nickname belongs to trail culture, not the literature.
- NSAID harm is not uniform: the desert RCT is positive while two single-dose heat-exercise trials are null — dose, duration, and hydration state are unresolved modifiers.
- CK thresholds do not predict AKI in exertional rhabdomyolysis (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39136944/, accessed 2026-11-15), and the CK >5000 U/L cutoff is routinely exceeded by asymptomatic recruits (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202487/; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22334169/, accessed 2026-11-15).
- Long-term renal consequences of repeated exertional AKI (CKD risk in athletes) remain unknown; the endurance-rhabdomyolysis review explicitly flags this gap (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32202487/, accessed 2026-11-15).

## 6. Open questions for the parent

- Frame the thru-hike as a *multistage* model: the BIERS/Lipman data support recurrent-but-resolving daily renal stress over weeks; index any carnitine-support claim to that cycle, not to single-event AKI.
- Position carnitine adjuncts against the baseline protections documented in §2f.
- Handle ibuprofen evidence honestly: strong repeated-dose RCT signal in desert ultras (NNH 5.5) but null single-dose heat-exercise trials; NSAID avoidance is also the cleanest modifiable-behavior lever for a trail-clinic audience.
- EAH is the counterintuitive desert failure mode: hydration advice must pair water with solute intake — the Grand Canyon case (Na 114 mmol/L with rhabdomyolysis and AKI) is the teachable example.
- For limitations: no renal biomarker data exist for actual thru-hikers. The 25 findings claims are quote-verified and liftable directly.
