\section{The Renal-Support File: Direct Evidence, Three Tiers}
\label{sec:support}

\subsection{Tier one --- regulatory precedent: replacement in failure states}

The one setting in which regulatory medicine formally prescribes carnitine for renal patients is instructive for what it is and is not. The US label for levocarnitine injection lists the ``prevention and treatment of carnitine deficiency in patients with end stage renal disease who are undergoing dialysis'' at 10--20 mg/kg dry weight after each session, with the pharmacology text explaining the mechanism of depletion: reduced intake, reduced renal synthesis, and dialytic losses.\src{66} The epidemiology behind that label is solid: single sessions strip 64--75\% of serum free carnitine, 25.3\% of 150 surveyed maintenance-dialysis patients were frankly deficient and 86.7\% insufficient by acyl/free ratio, and pharmacokinetic modeling shows the ongoing dialytic loss slowly depleting the whole-body pool between sessions.\src{94}\src{201}\src{200}

The trial record under that label is mixed, and this paper reports the mixture. Levocarnitine improved left-ventricular mass index, NT-proBNP, and erythropoietin responsiveness in hemodialysis patients with LVH; a 2026 double-blind pilot cut the erythropoietin-resistance index by 25\% versus 3\%; a systematic review found LDL and CRP reductions without confirmed hemoglobin or EPO-dose benefit.\src{198}\src{199}\src{197} But the 2022 Cochrane review across 52 RCTs and 3{,}398 dialysis participants ``does not currently support the use of carnitine supplementation in the treatment of dialysis-related carnitine deficiency.''\src{196} The precedent, stated precisely: carnitine replacement in renal failure is established pharmacology with contested outcomes --- it establishes that renal medicine considers this molecule safe enough to prescribe to its sickest patients, and not that supplementation improves their kidneys.

\subsection{Tier two --- mechanism and preclinical protection}

The mechanistic case is genuinely strong, and it begins with what the proximal tubule eats. The tubule ``use[s] fatty acid oxidation, which generates more adenosine triphosphate (ATP) than glucose metabolism, as its preferred metabolic pathway'' to power reabsorption --- the kidney is among the most mitochondria-dense, fat-burning organs in the body, and the gate on that fuel is carnitine.\src{214}\src{216} Ischemic acute renal failure is mechanistically a carnitine-pathway failure: $\beta$-oxidation is inhibited, long-chain acylcarnitines and acyl-CoA accumulate, and those accumulated esters inhibit proximal-tubule Na\textsuperscript{+}/K\textsuperscript{+}-ATPase --- the pump that does the tubule's work.\src{215} The intervention data follow the mechanism: L-carnitine pretreatment lowered malondialdehyde, myeloperoxidase, and nitrites after rat renal ischemia--reperfusion; pharmacologic stimulation of CPT1 (C75) recovered ATP, renal function, and tissue injury after I/R; carnitine blocked oxidative, mitochondrial, and apoptotic injury in human HK-2 proximal tubule cells; and it attenuated urographin-induced contrast nephrotoxicity in rats.\src{202}\src{204}\src{203}\src{206}

The most trail-relevant preclinical finding is the heat-stress AKI mouse study: L-carnitine pretreatment restored tubular mitochondrial ATP production and prevented the proinflammatory macrophage shift that drives heat-stress AKI --- the one experiment that joins this paper's three variables (carnitine, heat, kidney), in a mouse.\src{158} Patients with severe heat-related illness and AKI show elevated serum acylcarnitine, extending the metabolic signature to humans, associatively.\src{158} Under sustained exertion the acylcarnitine economy visibly works: Alaskan sled dogs on multiday submaximal exercise show chain-specific acylcarnitine shifts consistent with accelerated acyl-group export, and six months of endurance training in humans lowered muscle free and total carnitine while raising urinary esterified losses --- changes prevented by oral L-carnitine, and the cleanest available signal that sustained exertion taxes the carnitine economy at supplementable doses.\src{223}\src{222} Five-day fasting raised urinary carnitine excretion 4.6-fold in healthy men --- a calorie-deficit signal thru-hikers replicate.\src{221}

\subsection{Tier three --- clinical stress states: honest inventory}

Clinical trials of carnitine in renal stress exist, are small, and split. Contrast nephropathy: carnitine pretreatment prevented the urinary NGAL rise and reduced creatinine below baseline in CKD patients receiving contrast, and prevented the post-contrast plasma NGAL rise at 12 hours in 202 PCI patients --- surrogate endpoints only; no large outcome trial.\src{207}\src{208} Septic shock: a pilot RCT showed lower 28-day mortality (4/16 versus 9/15), but the 250-patient phase-2 RACE trial found 18 g levocarnitine did not meaningfully reduce organ failure, and meta-analysis found no significant mortality benefit.\src{210}\src{209}\src{211} Transplant ischemia--reperfusion: a pilot RCT of oral carnitine around deceased-donor transplantation did not reduce delayed graft function, though 3-month graft loss favored the supplemented arm (1 versus 6 patients).\src{213} The strongest non-dialysis renal signal is a single-center randomized trial in peritoneal-dialysis patients in which L-carnitine supplementation preserved residual renal function and increased urine volume, correlated with tubular-injury improvement --- one trial, single-center, hypothesis-raising.\src{217}

The bottom line of the three tiers, stated without inflation: carnitine replacement when the kidney is failing is established medicine with contested outcomes; carnitine as prophylaxis for a stressed but working kidney is mechanistically coherent (a $\beta$-oxidation organ, a carnitine-gated fuel pathway, preclinical protection in ischemia and heat) but has never been tested with a hard renal primary endpoint, and the null trials are part of the record.\src{209}\src{213}\src{196} No carnitine randomized trial in the exertion-heat-dehydration state exists at any dose.\src{246}

\section{The Counterweight: TMAO, Mapped at Full Strength}
\label{sec:tmao}

\subsection{The mechanism is not in dispute}

Oral L-carnitine, like choline, is converted by gut microbiota to trimethylamine, which hepatic FMO3 oxidizes to trimethylamine N-oxide; antibiotic suppression abolishes the post-challenge TMAO rise, which reappears after withdrawal.\src{238} The founding human work showed omnivores produce far more TMAO from a carnitine challenge than vegans or vegetarians, through a microbiota-dependent mechanism; in atherosclerosis-prone mice, chronic carnitine feeding increased TMA/TMAO synthesis and atherosclerosis only with intact microbiota, and in 2{,}595 cardiac-evaluation subjects plasma carnitine predicted incident cardiac events only among those with concurrently high TMAO.\src{225} The pathway is inducible and individual: omnivores show a $>$20-fold advantage in labelled-TMAO generation over vegetarians, the microbial $\gamma$-butyrobetaine step is induced by omnivorous diets and chronic carnitine exposure, omnivores carry 10-fold higher odds of the high-TMAO-producer phenotype, and microbiome classifiers predict producer status.\src{227}\src{228}\src{229} Most directly for this paper: at supplement doses --- 1--4 g/day for 12 or 24 weeks across 11 trials in healthy subjects --- L-carnitine raises fasting plasma TMAO without detectable inflammatory or oxidative-stress changes.\src{147}

\subsection{The kidney link, and where it bends}

TMAO is renally cleared and accumulates as filtration falls: dialysis patients show median TMAO of 94.4 $\mu$M versus 3.3 $\mu$M in controls, GFR is the dominant variable affecting TMAO in CKD 3--5, and successful transplantation reduces levels toward those of controls.\src{231}\src{230} The association literature is substantial: among 521 stable CKD subjects, top-quartile TMAO carried a 2.8-fold mortality risk; in 10{,}564 community adults, higher serial TMAO predicted incident CKD (quintile hazard ratios to 2.24) and faster annualized eGFR decline with monotonic dose--response; in human renal fibroblasts TMAO promoted fibrotic activation via PERK/Akt/mTOR and NLRP3 signaling; and in mice, elevated choline or TMAO led to progressive tubulointerstitial fibrosis.\src{232}\src{233}\src{235}\src{229} Cardiovascular associations parallel these: fasting TMAO predicted incident MACE in angiography cohorts, a 17-study meta-analysis found all-cause mortality HR 1.91 with 7.6\% risk rise per 10 $\mu$mol/L, and MESA showed dose-dependent incident ASCVD.\src{238}\src{239}\src{240}

Then the evidence bends, and this paper records the bend as carefully as the association. First, Mendelian randomization: genetically predicted TMAO and carnitine were not associated with T2DM, atrial fibrillation, CAD, MI, stroke, or CKD after correction, while diabetes and CKD causally raise TMAO --- the observational literature ``may be due to confounding or reverse causality.''\src{241} Second, the instructive null: among 1{,}243 maintenance-hemodialysis patients carrying the highest TMAO concentrations on record ($\sim$80\% at or above 56 $\mu$M, maximum 1{,}103 $\mu$M), there was no evidence linking TMAO to adverse cardiovascular outcomes or all-cause mortality --- at the extremes of exposure and renal failure, the association disappears.\src{234} Third, the fish problem: fish meals produce next-morning TMAO above the 6.4 $\mu$M MACE-associated cut-off in 45\% of fish-eaters versus 20\% after red meat, two randomized trials showed ``healthy'' diets (marine omega-3, whole-grain) significantly increased plasma TMAO, and reviewers conclude TMAO ``is not a universally valid biomarker of cardiometabolic risk independent of the background diet.''\src{244}\src{245}\src{249} Fourth, one direct mouse contradiction: high-dose L-carnitine feeding in ApoE$-/-$CETP mice left TMAO inversely correlated with aortic lesion size.\src{242} And fifth, the one carnitine atherosclerosis-outcome RCT --- 2 g/day for 6 months in metabolic syndrome --- was null on its carotid-volume primary endpoint, though the carnitine arm showed a 9.3\% greater increase in carotid stenosis plus higher LDL, a concerning secondary the paper carries rather than buries.\src{243}

\subsection{Translation to a healthy hiker on 2 grams a day}

The honest position has three parts. Established: microbiome-dependent production, omnivore/vegetarian divergence, and a fasting-TMAO rise at supplement doses.\src{225}\src{227}\src{147} Association-only: cardiovascular and CKD outcomes, contested by Mendelian randomization, an extreme-exposure null, and the fish dissociation.\src{239}\src{241}\src{234}\src{245} Not established: harm --- renal or cardiovascular --- from oral carnitine at supplement doses in healthy people with normal kidney function; the renal-safety review states plainly that athletes' renal risks and benefits of L-carnitine ``have not been evaluated yet,'' while carnitine up to 6{,}000 mg/day is generally considered safe in healthy adults.\src{246} Trained-population data are thin but pointed: in endurance-trained versus sedentary males, high-fat challenges evoked no detectable plasma-TMAO change despite higher precursor intake in the trained group, and a 2026 review finds TMAO does not consistently correlate with aerobic fitness, activity, or training status.\src{76}\src{247} One more form-specific fact the practical section needs: acetylcarnitine has lower bioavailability than carnitine, and $\sim$90\% of both was metabolized to TMAO in one PK study, reaching $\sim$50 $\mu$M plasma levels --- a reason the trail default is LCLT, not mega-dosed acetyl-carnitine.\src{266}

Dose framing, stated with both numbers: the formal risk assessment sets the observed safe level at 2{,}000 mg/day for chronic supplementation, noting data above that level are insufficient for confident long-term conclusions --- so 2--3 g/day sits slightly above the formally documented level and far below the ``generally considered safe'' 6{,}000 mg/day; the trail protocol below stays at 2 g.\src{248}\src{246} For a clinician wanting a number on a chronic supplementer, a fasting plasma TMAO at baseline and after 8--12 weeks on the intended dose is the measurable check, drawn fish-free for 24--48 hours; no outcome trial justifies intervening on the number itself.\src{244} And the one population caution this stream enforces: chronic supplementation in a hiker with established CKD is where the TMAO literature raises real caution --- the oral label itself warns that chronic high doses in severe renal impairment accumulate TMA and TMAO.\src{69}\src{241}

\section{The Practical Protocol: Dose, Form, Cautions, Logistics}
\label{sec:practical}

\subsection{The trail default}

The on-disk evidence supports one working answer: \textbf{L-carnitine L-tartrate powder at 2 g/day, split into two doses taken with carbohydrate-containing meals} --- the form and dose with the most consistent recovery data, at $\sim$\$0.13/day as bulk powder (\$65.97/kg = 500 two-gram servings; a 150-day hike consumes 300 g), roughly half the per-gram cost of capsules, and lighter in the pack.\src{267}\src{149}\src{272}\src{273} The split-dose rationale is pharmacokinetic, not ritual: only 14--18\% of a supplemental dose is absorbed, plasma does not rise further above 0.5 g three times daily, and the FDA label itself divides the adult dose (990 mg two or three times daily) around the same saturation.\src{116}\src{269}\src{69} Taking it with meals recruits the insulin gate the muscle-loading evidence requires; chasing it with caffeine accelerates clearance and protein co-ingestion blunts uptake, so the loading drink is carnitine plus carbohydrate, not plus a protein bar.\src{138}\src{114}\src{85} Acute dosing buys nothing; single doses move plasma, not muscle.\src{144}\src{146}

Other forms, briefly: acetyl-L-carnitine is the CNS-endpoint form (depressive-symptom meta-analysis at 1--3 g/day) but carries lower bioavailability and the TMAO-quantity concern above, so it has no trail advantage here.\src{256}\src{266} Glycine propionyl-L-carnitine produced one acute anaerobic-work signal (4.5 g pre-sprint) --- irrelevant to submaximal hiking.\src{264}

\subsection{Tolerability and the D,L lesson}

No authority has set a tolerable upper intake level; $\sim$3 g/day can cause nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhea, and the fishy body odor of trimethylamine, and the label notes dose reduction eliminates the symptoms --- tolerance should be watched in the first week and after increases.\src{6}\src{69} The historical scare is a chemistry lesson, not an L-carnitine warning: the myasthenia-like syndrome of the 1979--1984 literature occurred in dialysis patients receiving that era's \emph{D,L}-carnitine preparations, and the FDA label states mild myasthenia has been described ``only in uremic patients receiving D,L-carnitine'' --- modern products specify the L-isomer or L-tartrate, and a trail formulary should too.\src{262}\src{257}\src{261}\src{69}

\subsection{The four cautions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Coumarin anticoagulants.} The label directs INR monitoring when levocarnitine is started or adjusted in warfarin patients; the anchor case is a 33-year-old on acenocoumarol, stable for a year, whose INR rose to 4.65 after ten weeks of 1{,}000 mg/day carnitine in a fitness drink and restabilized off the supplement; one ICU RCT found 3 g/day \emph{decreased} INR --- direction is unpredictable, monitoring is not optional, and an anticoagulated hiker needs an INR check within two weeks of starting.\src{69}\src{65}\src{253}\src{255}\src{265}
\item \textbf{Thyroid-hormone replacement.} L-carnitine is a naturally occurring peripheral antagonist of thyroid hormone action, validated at 2--4 g/day in a randomized double-blind trial in TSH-suppressed women (reversing hyperthyroid symptoms, benefiting bone) --- a therapeutic feature in hyperthyroidism, a hazard for a levothyroxine-replacement hiker at sustained gram doses.\src{259}\src{258}
\item \textbf{Seizure disorders.} Seizures have been reported with oral and intravenous levocarnitine, with increased frequency or severity in patients with existing seizures and no dose threshold given; flag epilepsy rather than set limits.\src{69}
\item \textbf{CKD.} Oral levocarnitine ``has not been evaluated in patients with renal insufficiency,'' the label warns chronic high doses in renal impairment accumulate TMA/TMAO, a CKD rat study worsened disease progression, and Cochrane does not support routine dialysis supplementation --- the paradox (dialysis patients have the one approved carnitine indication) is exactly why CKD hikers should not self-dose without nephrology input.\src{69}\src{260}\src{196}\src{66}
\end{itemize}

Pregnancy and lactation: no adequate human studies; the label sanctions use only if clearly needed --- the trail formulary simply excludes it.\src{69}

\subsection{The logistics gaps, printed as gaps}

Two things this review could not source and will not invent: the heat stability of dry LCLT powder at desert pack temperatures has no data (nearest analogs are acetyl-carnitine solution stability and acylcarnitine degradation in stored dried blood spots --- neither approximates 40\,\textdegree C dry storage, so cool/dry/sealed is an unvalidated precaution), and independent label-accuracy surveys of commercial carnitine products were not found.\src{276}\src{275} A 150-day hike at 2 g/day is 300 g of powder or 600 capsules; the powder wins on cost and pack weight, and ``assay-verified'' on a label remains marketing until a survey says otherwise.\src{272}\src{273}

\section{Synthesis: The Trail Clinic View}
\label{sec:synth}

The unifying model this evidence supports: a desert thru-hike is a repeated, mostly resolving renal stress whose modifiable cofactors are hydration-with-solutes, NSAID avoidance, eccentric pacing, and acclimatization; and the carnitine economy --- a renal-conservation system gating the $\beta$-oxidation that powers the tubule --- is the metabolic subsystem most directly connected to that stress, in a body running a large energy deficit where carnitine's shuttle role is at home.\src{167}\src{168}\src{91}\src{214}\src{155} What can be said to a hiker, in tiers:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Defensible now:} 2 g/day LCLT split with meals is safe and well tolerated below $\sim$3 g/day; it reduces biochemical and MRI markers of exercise muscle damage in humans (reducing the substrate of rhabdomyolysis, the injury pathway most dangerous to a desert kidney); it costs thirteen cents a day; and the kidney--carnitine economy it supports is real, saturable, and renal.\src{6}\src{267}\src{18}\src{91}
\item \textbf{Plausible but unproven:} renal protection during exertion-heat stress (mechanistic depth plus mouse heat-AKI data, zero human trials); glycogen sparing at hiking intensity (measured in fed recreational athletes, not multiweek deficits); benefits beyond resistance-exercise contexts.\src{215}\src{158}\src{135}
\item \textbf{Not established:} any thru-hike-specific effect; any performance gain; sweat or dehydration effects on carnitine status; TMAO harm in healthy supplement users.\src{188}\src{144}\src{246}
\end{itemize}

The water question deserves its own paragraph because the client asked it directly, and the literature answers it with a boundary rather than a finding. Carnitine does not spare water: no evidence suggests supplementation changes sweat rate, thirst, or water requirements, and the renal carnitine economy's response to dehydration is simply untested.\src{128} What the water-limited hiker actually has is the stressor file's lesson --- the injury gradient runs through hydration state, NSAIDs, and pacing, and the EAH file's warning that overdrinking pure water in the heat creates its own encephalopathy --- so the renal plan is water \emph{with electrolytes}, ibuprofen retired, modest first weeks, and carnitine as the metabolic adjunct on top of that, not instead of it.\src{181}\src{177}\src{190}\src{179}

Monitoring is proportionate: nothing for a healthy hiker beyond symptom awareness; a fasting TMAO at baseline and 8--12 weeks for the curious (fish-free draw); INR within two weeks for anticoagulated users; and the exclusion list --- warfarin until checked, T4 replacement at gram doses, seizure disorders, CKD, pregnancy --- from the label file.\src{69}\src{65}\src{258}

The order of enquiry, cheapest first: (1) a field trial of 2 g/day LCLT in multistage desert ultramarathoners or supported thru-hikers with NGAL/KIM-1/creatinine endpoints --- the single study that would convert the preclinical heat-AKI and I/R file into an answer; (2) quantification of the chronic fasting-TMAO increment at 2 g/day in healthy athletes (currently unquantified in either direction); (3) a PK-bridged assessment of renal carnitine handling under dehydration; (4) assay of LCLT stability at pack temperatures; (5) replication of the muscle-damage effect in a loaded downhill-walking model.\src{147}\src{269}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

This review's boundaries are stated, not implied. No renal-function study of an actual multiweek thru-hike exists; every kidney inference is by analogy to multistage racing, occupational heat, military series, and park EMS data.\src{188} Whether endurance biomarker elevations represent structural injury or functional adaptation is explicitly unresolved in the primary literature, and the biomarker panels are not interchangeable with AKI diagnosis.\src{165}\src{166} The direct renal-protection file ends at preclinical models and surrogate endpoints; the human trials that exist are small, single-center, or null (RACE, delayed graft function, Cochrane's dialysis verdict), and no carnitine trial with a hard renal primary endpoint exists.\src{209}\src{213}\src{196} Dose translation is weak in both directions --- dialysis replacement dosing and sepsis megadosing (6--18 g IV) bracket the 2 g oral question without answering it.\src{66}\src{209} The TMAO counterweight is unresolved in its own literature: associations without established causation, contested by genetics, and unquantified for the specific population and dose in question.\src{241}\src{147} Trial-level cautions: the muscle-loading trial is n = 14 recreational athletes and unreplicated; one recovery trial is industry-funded with employee co-authors; two authors of a 2025 diabetes trial are Alfasigma employees; the 2026 performance review rated no included trial at low risk of bias.\src{135}\src{152}\src{144} And three practical gaps are carried as gaps: heat stability, label accuracy, and the ibuprofen ``vitamin I'' nickname --- which is documented trail culture, not indexed literature.\src{276}\src{182} Three of the six research streams died to a provider usage limit mid-run and were completed from saved sources; all quoted evidence passed the parent's mechanical re-verification regardless, but the event is part of the provenance record.
